A Chinese tea tin often carries four or five characters and nothing else. For someone who can read them, everything is sometimes there; for someone who cannot, there is usually less on it than they think. And on the European label the importer adds, there is almost always least of all.

This is what the words on a package do and do not guarantee.

## A mountain name is not a protected origin

Da Hong Pao, Long Jing, Tie Guan Yin, Bingdao: all four are place names or varietal names that have grown into product names. Geographic protection exists in China for some of them, but within China it is enforced patchily and outside China not at all.

In practice that means: if it says Long Jing, you know it is pan-fired green tea made in the Long Jing style. You do not know it comes from the West Lake area. The great majority of what is sold as Long Jing comes from Zhejiang but well outside Xi Hu, and a substantial share from Guizhou and Sichuan.

That is not fraud. It is the same as "cheddar": a style, not an address. It only becomes misleading when the price is the address's price.

## The words that do mean something

Some terms have a fairly fixed content in the trade.

**Zhengyan** on a Wuyi oolong points to the protected core area. It gets abused, but it is a checkable claim and a serious seller can tell you which gorge the leaf came from.

**Ming qian**, before Qingming, on green tea means picked before the Qingming festival in early April. That is a date, not a flavour, and therefore testable.

**Gu shu**, old tree, on pu-erh should mean the trees are older than a hundred years. This is the single most abused term in the Chinese tea trade. There is more gu shu on the market than there are gu shu trees.

**Tou chun**, first spring, and **chun cha**, spring tea, refer to the picking season and say something about concentration.

**Lao cong**, old bush, on Shui Xian means bushes of typically sixty years and older. This too is used generously.

## What Europe adds

The moment a batch enters Europe, a label is added that food law dictates. That label asks for ingredients, weight, shelf life and the details of the responsible business. It does not ask for harvest year, garden, altitude or producer.

Which explains why Belgian tea shops do not show that information: they are not obliged to, and often they do not have it themselves, because they buy through a European wholesaler who does not pass it on.

So it is usually not unwillingness. It is a chain in which those details drop out of the file somewhere between the garden and the shop.

## What we put on our pages, and why

Every tea on this site carries a passport: region, altitude, harvest year and month, garden or producer, cultivar, degree of oxidation, roast level and storage advice.

We can fill that in because we buy by the batch and ask the questions before we pay. It is not heroism; it is a purchasing method. Anyone buying from a wholesale catalogue cannot do it, however much they might want to.

And where we do not know, that is written too. Both our matchas note that Japanese producers do not report garden altitude. The vertical pu-erh set notes that one of the four vintages is not a pure garden batch, because the 2019 spring picking failed and the family bought leaf from the neighbours on the same slope.

That last one probably cost us sales. We think it matters more that it is accurate, because a passport is worth nothing the moment you have to guess which fields are filled in because they are true and which because they look good.

## Three questions you can ask anywhere

If you are buying elsewhere and want to know where you stand, these three always work.

Which year is this harvest from? With pu-erh and white tea this is the most important question there is; with green tea it decides whether it is still fresh.

Which garden or producer is it from? If the answer is "from China", you are buying a style and not a batch, and the price should match.

How many infusions does it take? This is the hardest to fake, because answering it requires that someone has brewed the tea themselves.
