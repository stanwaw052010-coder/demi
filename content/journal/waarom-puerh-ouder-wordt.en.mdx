There is one sentence you will hear in every tea shop the moment pu-erh comes up: it gets better with age. That is about as true as the claim that wine gets better with age, and true in exactly the same way: it holds for some bottles, under certain conditions, up to a point, and the rest is nonsense that sells.

What actually happens is worth knowing, because it decides whether the cake in your cupboard will be worth something in ten years or merely older.

## Why pu-erh is not finished

All tea gets killed at some point. In a pan, with steam, or with hot air — the aim is the same: switch off the enzymes in the leaf so oxidation stops and the tea stays as the maker intended. Green tea is killed hard and is done after that. From that moment it only declines, which is why our shelf says to drink our Long Jing this year.

Sheng pu-erh is deliberately killed *softly*. The pan is cooler and the time shorter, just enough to allow the leaf to be rolled, but not enough to switch everything off. Enzymatic activity remains. So does a living microflora: yeasts and fungi that come along from the garden and survive the pressing.

Those two together — residual enzymes and microbes — do the work over the years that follow. They break the heavy, bitter polyphenols down into smaller, sweeter compounds. Caffeine binds to breakdown products and is felt less sharply. The tea loses its green, prickling top layer and gets wood, camphor and a thickness back in return that you find in no young tea.

That is not romance. It is slow chemistry in a compressed disc of leaf.

## Why it usually goes wrong

The reaction needs moisture. Without it the microbes do nothing and the enzymes are too slow to mean anything within a human lifetime. A cake that has sat for ten years in a dry European living room at 40 percent humidity is, after ten years, almost exactly what it was, only a little flatter.

Too much moisture is worse. Above roughly 80 percent you grow mould you do not want, and then there is no way back. Between those two lies a window of roughly 60 to 70 percent relative humidity, at a temperature that does not swing too much.

There is a third way to lose it, and it is the most common one in Belgium: smell. Pu-erh breathes. A cake standing next to the coffee tin is, within a year, a cake that smells of coffee, and that never comes out again. The same goes for spices, soap and fresh timber.

## The curve is not a straight line

What sellers rarely mention: there is a trough. For the first two to three years a young sheng is lively and sharp and interesting. Then it drops. Somewhere between year three and year seven it loses its freshness before anything ripe has arrived to replace it. Chinese drinkers call this the quiet period, and it is precisely the phase in which people conclude they have been talked into something.

Then it comes back. Around year eight to ten the wood starts, and from about fifteen years there is a different tea in the cup than the one that was put away.

That is also why, in the Vault, we send samples in years three, five and eight. Not as a courtesy, but because otherwise you give away a cake during the trough without ever having tasted where it was heading.

## What decides whether a cake is worth it

Age adds nothing to leaf that had nothing to give. A cheap cake of factory material becomes, after fifteen years, an old cheap cake. What does count:

Leaf from older trees holds more dissolved compounds and so has more material to work with. Whole leaf ages more slowly and more evenly than broken leaf. A cake pressed firmly but not rock-hard lets air in without falling apart. And storage weighs more heavily than everything above put together: the same cake in Kunming and in Guangzhou are two different teas after ten years, and both camps have their adherents.
