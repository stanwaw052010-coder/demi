Almost every European tea shop carries a Da Hong Pao. It costs between eight and fifteen euro per hundred grams, it tastes roasted and pleasant, and there is nothing wrong with it. But it has little to do with what the word means in Wuyi.

The difference sits in one term that is rarely translated: *zhengyan*.

## Three rings on a map

The Wuyi area in northern Fujian is divided into zones, and that division is not folklore but administrative and strict.

**Zhengyan**, literally true rock, is the core area: roughly seventy square kilometres of sandstone gorges where the bushes stand between rock walls, often on narrow terraces of weathered stone and carried-in soil. Little sun, much mist, excellent drainage, and a soil rich in minerals from the eroding rock.

**Banyan**, half rock, is the rim around it. Flatter, more sun, ordinary ground.

**Zhoucha** or *waishan* is everything beyond that: the plain, and these days gardens in other provinces too.

The same varietal — Qi Dan, for instance, one of the cultivars sold as Da Hong Pao — grows in all three zones. In all three cases the product may be called Da Hong Pao. The prices differ by a factor of ten to fifty.

## What the rock does

The claim is that the stone is tasteable, which sounds like something sellers invent. There is nevertheless a physical mechanism.

The weathered sandstone drains water quickly while holding minerals the root takes up. The bushes stand in the shade of the gorge walls and therefore grow slowly, which shifts the ratio of amino acids to polyphenols. And the mist that hangs between the walls keeps the leaf temperature low.

The result is called *yan yun* in Chinese, rock rhyme. It is not a flavour but an aftertaste: a dry, cool, almost saline minerality that stays at the back of the mouth after the sweetness has gone, and that strengthens rather than fades across eight or nine infusions.

Whether you taste it depends on whether anyone has ever told you what to look for. It is not in the first two infusions — in a well-roasted yancha those are mostly fire. From the third it surfaces.

## The roasting, and why it goes wrong so easily

Yancha is roasted over charcoal, in round baskets, sometimes in several rounds spread over months. It is the most fragile step in the whole process.

Well-roasted leaf smells of roasted nuts, dried fruit and dark chocolate. The roast is then a layer *over* the tea, with the minerality coming through it. Badly roasted leaf smells of ash and charcoal, full stop. The roast is then all that is left.

And there is a commercial problem inside that: a heavy roast masks mediocre leaf very effectively. If you buy a yancha that tastes only of smoke, there is a fair chance that was the intention.

A good yancha needs rest after roasting. Straight out of the basket it is sharp and closed. Three to six months later the fire has settled and the tea has opened. Which is why we buy the spring harvest in autumn, not in June.

If someone sells you a Da Hong Pao without saying which zone it comes from, the answer is almost always waishan. That is no disgrace — it is decent tea. But then the name is a style, not an address, and you had best not pay more than a style is worth.
