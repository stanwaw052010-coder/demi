Nearly every European tea shop divides its shelf in four: black, green, white, and then a remainder category holding oolong and pu-erh. In China there are six, the division is different, and it rests on one criterion the European division does not use.

That criterion is not colour and not flavour. It is processing.

## It all comes from the same plant

Camellia sinensis. One species, with two important varieties: *sinensis*, small-leaved, from China and Japan, and *assamica*, broad-leaved, from Assam and Yunnan. Everything you drink comes from that plant. Rooibos, chamomile and mint are not tea, and the Chinese scheme accordingly leaves them out.

What separates the six types is what happens to the leaf *after* picking: how hard it is killed, how far it is allowed to oxidise, whether it is rolled, whether it is further fermented.

## The six

**Green tea.** Killed fast and hard, in a pan or with steam. Oxidation stops almost at once. What you taste is as close to fresh leaf as tea gets. Perishable: finish it within a year.

**Yellow tea.** Green tea with one extra step, men huang, the smothering: the warm leaf is wrapped and left for hours or days. The grassiness goes, chestnut and honey come back in its place. Labour intensive, low yield, and therefore nearly extinct.

**White tea.** The least processed of all: wither and dry. No pan, no rolling. Some oxidation occurs during the withering, usually between 5 and 15 percent.

**Oolong.** Partially oxidised, from about 20 to 60 percent, with the leaf edges bruised to steer the oxidation. Often roasted. This is the broadest category: a light Ali Shan and a heavy Da Hong Pao have almost nothing in common.

**Red tea.** What the West calls black tea. Fully oxidised, 85 to 95 percent. The liquor is red-brown, hence the Chinese name.

**Black tea, hei cha.** Post-fermented with the help of microbes, not merely oxidised. Pu-erh falls here, along with Liu Bao, Fu Zhuan and other pressed teas.

## Why pu-erh is a problem for the scheme

Sheng pu-erh is made like a sort of unfinished green tea, and could therefore sit under green. But it is meant to go on fermenting for years, and that is what defines hei cha.

Shou pu-erh is unambiguously hei cha: the wo dui, the forty to sixty day wet pile, is accelerated microbial fermentation.

Chinese literature resolves this in different ways. Some schemes put all pu-erh under hei cha; others treat sheng and shou separately. On this site we do the latter, because for someone who has to choose, the difference between sheng and shou is larger than the difference between green and white.

## Why the European division persists

Black, green and fruit is a division that follows from what was sold in Europe for a hundred years. Black tea came from India and Ceylon and was drunk with milk; green tea was a niche; oolong and pu-erh did not exist here.

So the division is not a mistake but a fossil: it accurately describes a range that no longer matches what is for sale.

The trouble is that the division steers what shops buy. If "oolong" is one shelf rather than a range from twenty to sixty percent oxidation, then two oolongs go on that shelf and the matter is settled. Half a tea world disappears from view that way, not through unwillingness but through a category system with no room for it.

So we sort at the source: sheng, shou, oolong, white, red, green, yellow. Seven entrances, because the two pu-erhs stand apart. And matcha, which is Japanese and a powder, gets its own collection rather than a slot on the green shelf.
