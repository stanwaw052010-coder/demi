Last year we had the same complaint three times, from three people none of whom were doing anything wrong. The tea they had tasted at our table tasted different at home. Flatter. Duller. For one of them, frankly metallic.

All three lived in a different municipality. All three used tap water.

## The sum nobody does

A 110 ml gongfu cup holds about 7 grams of leaf, of which a fraction of a gram dissolves per infusion. The rest, more than 99 percent of what you drink, is water.

We pay attention to the year, the garden, the altitude and the roast of that one percent, and then pour the cheapest liquid in the house on top of it without a thought. It is the most lopsided ratio in the whole of tea.

## What hardness does to tea

Flemish tap water is hard. Depending on where you live, total hardness runs roughly between 25 and 45 French degrees, which comes to a dry residue of some 300 to 500 milligrams per litre. That is mostly calcium and magnesium, plus bicarbonate.

Three things go wrong.

Calcium binds to catechins and to the aromatic compounds that make a tea fragrant. What binds does not reach your nose. The tea becomes literally less smellable, and because taste is largely smell, less flavourful too.

Bicarbonate raises the pH. Tea extraction is sensitive to acidity; above pH 8 the colour shifts dark and dull, and a green tea loses its clarity. You see that in the cup before you taste it.

And the film. That grey skin on cooled tea is not dirt but a compound of calcium with tea oils. On soft water it does not appear.

## What we measured

In the shop we brewed the same Huangshan Mao Feng on four kinds of water, all at 82 degrees, all 4 grams to 110 ml, same gaiwan, same timer.

**Ghent tap water, unfiltered.** Aroma nearly gone. The chestnut note that marks this tea was absent. Heavy, somewhat bitter in the finish. Clear film.

**The same water, through an ordinary filter jug.** Noticeably better. The aroma came back, not fully. No film. This is the cheapest sensible improvement there is.

**Spa Reine, dry residue 33 mg/l.** Clear and clean, and too thin. The tea lost body. This is the mirror image of hard water: without minerals not enough is extracted.

**Volvic, dry residue 109 mg/l.** The best of the four. Magnolia in the aroma, chestnut in the mouth, and the melon-like sweetness in the finish this tea is supposed to have.

We then did the same with a shou pu-erh. The difference was there too, but far smaller. Which makes sense: a dark, earthy tea does not have the fine top layer hard water takes away.

## Practical conclusion

For shou pu-erh, roasted oolong and red tea, Flemish tap water is good enough. For green tea, white tea, yellow tea, light oolong and young sheng it is not.

If you want to buy nothing: let the water stand in a jug overnight so it takes on air, and boil it once, not twice. Reboiling concentrates what was already in it.

If you want to do something that genuinely helps: a twenty-five euro filter jug. It is the cheapest way to make your entire tea shelf taste better, and it costs less than one packet of our Long Jing.

And if you want the maximum: bottled Volvic, or a reverse osmosis filter where you redose the minerals yourself. That last one is a hobby in itself, and we entirely understand if you do not start.

But do not start with more expensive tea if your water is the problem. That is the costliest way to solve nothing.
